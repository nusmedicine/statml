Maximum likelihood — what the figure has to show

For widget 8. The claim under test: the likelihood is built the same way every pile in this collection is built — one value at a time, except the values are not random draws, they are candidates you tried. Each press asks one question — how probable is what I actually saw, if this were the parameter? — and drops the answer into the panel below.

The top panel is one point of the bottom panel. A candidate puts a model over the data; every observation gets a height under it; the twelve heights multiply to one number; that number is one dot below. Slide the candidate and you are walking along the bottom panel's x-axis.

The bottom panel's y-axis carries the misconception target in its label: it is P(the data | the parameter), not a distribution over the parameter. Watch its exponent when you change n — a probability distribution cannot lose ten orders of magnitude because you collected more data.

Both numbers are printed on the panels. The top one reads 1.000 exactly, because for counts it is a sum of genuine probabilities. The bottom one reads whatever it reads, and changing the range you swept changes it — which is the point: there is no natural total.

  1. Does panel A read as “this candidate explains these points badly”? Drag the candidate to an end of its range. The stems should visibly collapse. If they do not, the multiplication is invisible and the mechanism is not on screen.
  2. Counts or measurements? The lesson uses a negative binomial and the domain is RNA-seq, but the discrete pmf is flatter and its duplicate values stack on the same stem. The normal is instantly readable and costs the exact phrase “probability of the data” — its heights are densities. Pick one.
  3. Does the contribution row earn its 80 px? It is the only mark that shows all n factors separately, on a common scale, with duplicates visible. It is also a third panel. Turn it off and see what is lost.
  4. Likelihood or log-likelihood? The raw one carries the honest number and an absurd exponent. The log one is what optim actually maximises and is the only one still legible at n = 60. The peak is in the same place — which is worth a sentence in the widget.
  5. Is the peak findable by eye? If the curve is flat near its maximum, “read off the top” is not a thing a student can do — which is an argument for the widget, not against it, but the figure has to make the flatness visible rather than hide it.
  6. Does the curve tighten with n? Step n through 6 → 60 with a completed sweep. If the narrowing is obvious, widget 9's posterior has its setup for free.
  7. Do the two “adds to” numbers land? Top panel: 1.000. Bottom panel: some absurd total that moves when you sweep a different range. That pair is the entire probability-vs-likelihood distinction, stated in the only place a student is already looking. If it does not read, it wants prose instead — but prose is what everyone already tries.
  8. Should the true distribution be shown at all? Switch it on above. In the widget it would not be a toggle: it is what the Draw the sample lead shows while the observations fall out of it, and then it goes for good — the one time the arrow runs forwards. Everything after that runs backwards. Judge whether seeing it once, and losing it, carries that.
  9. Should the dots be joined before the sweep finishes? Here neighbouring visited points are joined and nothing else — a line across a gap would be a claim about a candidate nobody evaluated.

Stage two — both parameters unknown

The same counts, the same likelihood, one more unknown: the dispersion. The curve becomes a surface and there is no formula for the peak — which is exactly why the lesson picked this model. Darker is more likely; the bands are drops of 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20 in log-likelihood from the maximum.

What to look for: the two parameters are not equally determined. Measured at n = 12, seed 3: the mean is pinned inside about 7–11.5, a factor of 1.6; the dispersion runs from about 0.8 to 4, a factor of 5 — on a log axis, which is the honest scale for it. The MLE lands at 1.31 against a true 2.5 and the surface barely objects. That is not a drawing artefact — it is why edgeR and DESeq2 shrink dispersion estimates across genes rather than trust the per-gene MLE. Change n above and watch the ridge close up.

  1. Is the banana legible as a shape, or does it just read as a blur? Stepped bands were chosen over a smooth heatmap so the contour is countable. Judge that.
  2. Does the grid cost land? 41 evaluations built the curve above; this is 96 × 72 = 6,912 for a worse answer. That gap is the argument that MLE is a search rather than a sweep — but it is currently only available as a sentence, not as something you watch.
  3. Is the truth marker honest or distracting? We know the true parameters because the population is seeded (widget 3's move). The MLE lands somewhere else, which is the point.
