  • What the MCMC tab should show Settled: B, and hollow rings. For widget 9. MCMC does not compute the normalising constant — it avoids it, because P(counts) cancels out of the acceptance ratio. Three layouts for making that cancellation visible rather than asserted; the two-bar version shipped, and the histogram of the draws was cut because at forty draws it reads as the sampler failing rather than working slowly. Also three ways to show the counts a lead has dealt but Step has not reached: rings won, because at n = 60 a faint dot is indistinguishable from a solid one in the same column.
  • How wide should a subtitle be Open question. Answers “why not let the subtitle go full width instead of leaving the right half empty?” with the number behind it: full width is 196 characters per line, about 2.6× the readable maximum. But it also shows the shipping cap is already past the band at 82, not short of it — so the fix is a narrower measure, not a wider one, and the empty space is answered by shorter copy rather than by stretching text into it. Reads the live subtitle out of the widget, so it cannot drift.
  • The null and the p-value For widget 5. Tests whether “a dot that lands in a tail stays lit” makes p countable rather than an abstract area. A real effect beside no effect at all, so the false positive is one click away.
  • Where do the numbers come from? Settled: scope A, in two stages. For widget 11. Stage 1 asks how did these numbers get made? and plays the notebook's four count distributions as generative mechanisms — in two pairs, where only one thing changes between the panels of a pair: fixed chance vs moving chance, fixed rate vs redrawn rate. Stage 2 asks which one is my data? as a four-question decision tree, none of the questions about the shape of a histogram, landing on the distribution's R code and figure with d/p/q/r read off it. Also carries the measurement that fixed the framing: Poisson against negative binomial separates 3.5× at the notebook's own numbers, binomial against hypergeometric only 5% — so the second pair's lesson is “the chance moves as the pool empties”, not “without replacement is narrower”.
  • Two tabs, and a live decision tree Proposed restructure of widget 11. Distributions shows the whole tree at once — eight endings visible from the first click — and lights up the path as it is walked, ending on a nameplate: name, discrete/continuous, what it counts, parameters, R call. R code turns each of d/p/q/r into the question it answers, with its own picture. Four discrete and four continuous. Normal against log-normal is settled by a test needing no data — could the biggest plausible value be ten times the smallest? — since being a lab value settles nothing: serum sodium spans 1.1×, CRP spans 105×.
  • The denominator that moves Settled: A, with the division written out. For widget 12. Risk and odds share a numerator and differ in denominator, so the odds ratio always overstates the risk ratio — by 1% at a 1% base rate and 200% at 40%, with the effect pinned at RR = 2.00 throughout. Four treatments of the one motion that is not settled by arithmetic: do the survivors step aside, or is a bracket enough, or are the 200 people furniture? Candidate D is the 2×2 and four numbers — what every existing tool does — on the page as the baseline to beat. A won and carries the fraction underneath, whose denominator sheds its red dots one at a time as the reading changes: risk's numerator sits inside its denominator, odds' sits beside it, and that is why only one of the two can pass 1.
  • A simpler 2×2 Widget 12, rethought after review. Three complaints answered at once: the view buttons were section headings pretending to be controls, “true risk ratio” implied a sampling story that does not exist here, and the ratios were inputs when a ratio should always be an output. A gives you the four counts on sliders and computes both ratios in front of you. B asks which side of the table did you fill in first: the numbers you chose are boxed, the numbers you counted are not, and enrolling fewer survivors drives your study's death rate to 81% and the relative risk to 1.22 while the odds ratio sits at 2.67 throughout.
