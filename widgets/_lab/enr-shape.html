Slot 5 · one widget with two tabs, or one stage carrying both

The open call is the shape: ORA and GSEA as two tabs of one widget, or as two widgets. node widgets/_lab/enr-measure.mjs answered the substance first, and the answer bears on the shape: every claim that survived measurement is a statement about one ranked list scored two ways. ORA's verdict moves with a cutoff and a background nobody chose; GSEA needs neither. Split across two widgets, not one of those comparisons can be made.

So the real choice is narrower than the call assumed — two tabs, or one stage showing both at once. Both are drawn below at the true figure width (770 px, measured on a live widget), on the real engine. Move the cutoff and watch which numbers move.

Shape A — two tabs on one shared list

Each method gets the whole figure. The tab you are not on is not on screen.

Costs: the comparison is a memory test. You move the cutoff on the ORA tab, switch, and have to remember what the score was. Buys: the 2×2 is big enough to read from the back of a room, and each tab is one idea.

Shape B — one stage, both methods on it

One cutoff line through one ranked list. Everything above the line is ORA's; everything below it is GSEA's.

Costs: denser, and the 2×2 shrinks to a strip. Buys: the whole claim is one gesture. Drag the cutoff and the p-value swings while the curve underneath does not move a pixel.

Shape C — one stage, three strips on one axis, numbers in the readout

Shape B with its two answer panels taken off the canvas. The figure becomes what it actually is — three readings of one ranked list, sharing one x axis — and the numbers go where every other widget in this collection puts them, in the readout tiles under the figure. The 2×2 and the permutation null are then a gated second stage rather than permanent furniture.

Costs: the 2×2 is behind a gate, so the mechanism of ORA is one click away rather than on screen. Buys: every stroke on the canvas is the ranked list, the strips are twice as tall as in shape B, and the numbers are the size the readout draws them — which is the size that has survived projection in forty-two widgets.

The null is two humps, and that is correct. The score is the largest deviation of a walk from zero, so a walk that never deviates is the rarest outcome, not the commonest — which is also why GSEA normalises the positive and negative sides separately before comparing sets. A student seeing this for the first time will ask; the widget should answer on screen.
