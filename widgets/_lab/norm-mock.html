Slot 1 candidate · normalization — the stage, the two panels, and the rail

PHM5003 05 - Introduction to High Throughput Data / 03 - Normalization and Transformation. The misconception: that "normalising" makes data normal — that scaling and transforming are one operation with one purpose. The lesson judges every method in the same two-panel picture (a boxplot per sample, a mean-versus-variance scatter over genes) and writes the verdict underneath as two bullets, Effect on: Distribution and Effect on: Mean-Variance Relationship. So the widget is those two panels, and the readouts are the notebook's own claims.

Everything below is drawn from _lab/norm-model.js — the engine that would ship, imported and not copied. Every number in a caption is computed on this page, live. node widgets/_lab/norm-measure.mjs prints the same values as a table.

§1 · THE STAGE — the notebook's own has nothing for a normaliser to correct

simulate_data draws every sample from the same mean vector, so the ten samples are exchangeable before anything is done to them. Their boxplots already line up: the raw medians span 32–37, which is sampling noise on 1000 genes and not a depth difference. A method whose whole job is putting samples on one scale is being shown on samples that are already on one scale.

spread gives sample j a depth factor — sequencing depth, or injected sample amount. Three stages below, each raw and then median-normalised.

ASK 1. Stage A (the notebook, spread = 0), stage B (0.5) or stage C (1.0)? B and C depart from the lesson's own figure; A is faithful and shows nothing being fixed. If B or C, is spread a slider the reader can turn to zero, or a fixed property of the stage?

§2 · PANEL 1 — three ways to draw "did the distribution move?"

Six states of the same data. A is the notebook's boxplot per sample. B overlays one density curve per sample. C overlays the ECDFs — the most direct picture of the distributions are now identical, which is quantile normalisation's one claim. All three drawn on stage B, at the width the widget's figure would get.

ASK 2. Boxplots, densities or ECDFs — and does quantile normalisation read as "identical" in the one you pick?

§3 · THE AXIS QUESTION — the lesson is that nothing happened, and an autoscale hides it

Min-max sends the data to [0, 1] and changes nothing else: the pooled skew is unchanged to 7e-14, which is float noise. That is the whole lesson, and it is also a drawing problem. Fit the axis and the picture is pixel-identical to raw, so a reader concludes the control is broken. Fix the axis and the scaled data collapses to an unreadable sliver at the bottom.

ASK 3. A (fit, with the range printed loudly), B (fixed), C (fit, with a range ribbon under the panel showing where the data used to sit), or D (a Same axes / Fit axes toggle — one control, and it does carry an idea)?

§4 · PANEL 2 — mean versus variance, and where the obvious readout breaks

The notebook plots raw mean against raw variance and the cloud is a fan. The obvious readout — the slope of log(variance) on log(mean) — is wrong twice over, and that is why the tiles print Spearman ρ instead. Both are drawn below with their numbers.

ASK 4. Raw axes (the notebook's) or log–log? Log–log makes the relationship a straight line you can see the slope of, and is undefined for 558 of 1000 genes the moment the reader picks z-score.

§5 · THE RAIL — one picker of ten, or two controls in sequence

This is the design claim, not a layout preference. The misconception is that scaling and transforming are one operation. Rail A offers one select of ten methods grouped the way the notebook groups them — the two operations are a heading. Rail B is two controls applied in order, so the reader can compose them (quantile, then log) and can see that neither does the other's job. The shape of the rail makes the argument before any caption does — principle 2.7.

Composition, measured on stage C. spread is what normalisation moves; skew and ρ are what transformation moves. No cell has both columns fixed by one control.

ASK 5. Rail A or rail B? And if B: Box-Cox λ visible always, or only when Box-Cox is chosen (when:)? Note Box-Cox is undefined at or below zero, so it cannot follow z-score — the rail has to say so somehow.

§6 · THE READOUT — three tiles, one per question

Each tile answers one of the panels' questions, and each is the notebook's own claim turned into a number. Shown at four states of rail B.

ASK 6. Three tiles, or two? Samples on one scale is the only one panel 1 can be read for directly; Skew and ρ are the notebook's two "Effect on:" bullets.

§7 · What is NOT on this page

The quantile procedure as an animation — rank, average across samples at each rank, assign back — is three numbered steps in cell 16 that no student can picture, and it is a countable thing while the count is small (2.3). It is the obvious second act and it is deliberately not mocked here, because the first six asks decide the stage it would animate. TPM is also absent: it needs a gene-length column the stage does not have, and its formula is a within-sample ratio the median tile already shows.
