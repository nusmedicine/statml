Two curves, then the surface — where do they go?

Widget 8, stages two and three. The proposal: before the contour, search the mean and the spread as two separate likelihood curves. The contour then arrives as the thing that connects them rather than as a new kind of object.

The two curves have a real payload of their own, and it is clearer as two curves than as any contour: the mean's peak is sharp and the spread's is flabby. You can find one of these parameters and you cannot really find the other. Both curves peak at exactly the same height — the joint maximum — so they can honestly share a y-axis, which is what makes the comparison fair.

And the contour has a payload the curves cannot carry. Measured on this data: the best mean is 9.165 whatever spread you assume — 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, 20, all give the same answer, because for a fixed spread the negative binomial's MLE for the mean is the sample mean. So the crest of the surface is a vertical line. But it does not work the other way: assume a mean of 5 and the best spread is 0.87; assume 9.17 and it is 1.35. One parameter is immune to what you guess about the other; the other is not. That asymmetry is a shape, and only the surface has shapes.

The layout criteria, since this is a layout question

Alignment
A shared axis should be physically shared. The mean curve's x-axis and the contour's x-axis are the same quantity; if they are not on the same pixels, the reader has to be told they match instead of seeing it.
Constancy
Between stages, a mark that means the same thing should not move. Every pixel that moves is a re-orientation the reader pays for.
Comparability
Stage two's job is comparing two peaks. Two curves in the same orientation compare far better than one upright and one rotated.
Figure and ground
The contour is dense ink; the curves are sparse. The dense thing will dominate whatever you do, so it should not also be first in reading order.

Constancy and comparability pull against each other here, and that is the whole decision. A marginal curve on the surface's y-axis has to be rotated; a curve you are comparing peaks with should not be. Something has to give in stage two or in stage three.

  1. Flip the stage toggle and watch what moves. That is the test. In A nothing moves at all; in B the spread curve rotates and crosses the panel; in C nothing moves but nothing lines up either.
  2. Can you read the rotated curve? A is asking you to compare a sharp upright peak with a sharp sideways one, in stage two, with no contour between them yet. If that comparison fails, A's constancy was bought with the thing stage two exists to do.
  3. Does A's empty cell read as a hole or as a question? It is captioned as a question — "do these two answers depend on each other?" — which is the honest description of what goes there.
  4. In C, is the unshared mean axis actually a problem? The contour's mean axis runs the full width while the mean curve above it runs half. They are the same quantity at two different scales, one above the other, which is the exact configuration that invites a false reading.
  5. Height. Printed under each. The widget's canvas is already 604 px in stage one; whatever this costs is on top of the model panel, the data strip and the factor row.
  6. Does the vertical crest read in the contour? That is the stage-three payload. If the bands are too soft to see that the ridge runs straight up, the fact needs a mark of its own rather than a shape.
