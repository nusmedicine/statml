The two kernels — four ways, and which of them can show the KL

Kenneth asked two things: can the Gaussian and the t be shown overlapping for a sample you click, and does that help visualise the KL divergence. Four variants below, on the widget’s own stage — 48 samples, four groups, perplexity 5. Click any sample in the cloud at the top and every panel follows it.

The research, briefly. The two-curves-overlaid idiom is not from the t-SNE paper — van der Maaten & Hinton’s Figure 1 is a gradient heatmap, high-dimensional distance against low-dimensional distance, coloured by the force between a pair. That is variant D. The curve overlay is the teaching idiom, and it is what PHM5003 cell 40 uses, drawn as two separate figures on two different y-scales.

The stage — click a sample

The same generated cloud the widget uses. Left, the samples in three genes; right, the t-SNE arrangement after the full 1000 steps. The ringed sample is the one every panel below is about.

A · the two curves on one axis, with this sample’s real neighbours on them

Kenneth’s ask, drawn literally

The Gaussian is this sample’s own σ — the one perplexity chose for it — over real 3-D distance. The t is over real distance in the picture. Every other sample is a tick on each curve, at its actual distance, so you can see which ones the kernel counts as neighbours.

The honesty cost, and it is why this needs your eye: the two x-axes are not the same quantity. One is a distance in gene space, the other a distance in a picture whose scale is arbitrary. To put them on one axis the picture is rescaled so its median pair distance matches the data’s. That changes no ranking and no conclusion, but the t curve you see is drawn at a scale the algorithm never used.

B · the same two curves, each in units of its own width

No rescaling, and the axes are honest — but they mean different things

Each curve is drawn against distance measured in units of that kernel’s own width: the Gaussian in units of σi, the t in its own units, where the kernel’s width is fixed at 1. That is the natural dimensionless variable for each, so nothing is invented — and the tail difference is the only thing left to see.

The ticks are the same samples as in A. Watch where they pile up: in the data they sit far out in the Gaussian’s dead zone, and in the picture they sit where the t still has probability to give. That gap is the crowding problem, which is the whole reason for the heavy tail.

C · the bars, with the KL’s own terms under them

What the widget has now, plus the thing that actually is the KL

Top: the two distributions, as the widget draws them. Bottom: the signed per-neighbour term, p·log(p/q) — and the KL is exactly the sum of those, no proxy. Above the line costs; below the line is a credit.

The trap this exists to avoid: shading the gap between the outline and the bar would be wrong. The gap is |p−q|, but the terms are signed and log-weighted, so a pair the picture over-weights points downwards. Shading would show a cost where the truth is a credit.

D · the paper’s own figure — the force between a pair

van der Maaten & Hinton, Figure 1

Distance in the data across, distance in the picture up, and the colour is the gradient — which way and how hard t-SNE pulls that pair. Blue pulls together, red pushes apart. It answers a question the curves cannot: not “what is the similarity” but “what will the method DO about it”. The ringed sample’s pairs are plotted on it as dots, so you can see where this sample’s own forces sit.

Where this leaves it

Does the kernel diagram help visualise the KL? No — and A and B both show why. The kernel turns a distance into a similarity; the KL compares two distributions, which is what you get after normalising. The curves are one step upstream, and no amount of overlaying reaches the divergence.

What the curves DO explain is the one thing the bars cannot: why q keeps probability out in the tail, and therefore why pulling a true neighbour apart is expensive while drawing a stranger close is free. Measured on this stage: pairs the picture under-weights carry 137% of the KL, pairs it over-weights carry −37%, and the worst 2% of pairs carry 77% of the total.

So they are complementary, not alternatives. B is the honest version of the overlay and it is small enough to be an inset rather than a quadrant. C is the one that shows the KL. D is the paper’s own and answers a different question again.
