The page on the widget's own cells (seed 1, resolution 0.3), with every figure computed as the widget does: FindMarkers over cells, DESeq2 over the four pseudobulk samples. Every variant is live: click a cluster on the map, a point on a volcano or a row of a list to pick the gene, and the bar's Sum each sample runs the press in all four at once.

What the layouts are judged against

  • Overview first, then details on demand (Shneiderman's visual-information-seeking mantra). The volcanos are the overview, one gene is the detail; the detail belongs after the thing it is picked from. Your "move it below the volcanos" is this.
  • Spatial contiguity and signalling (Mayer's multimedia principles): a result sits next to what explains it; the reader should not travel between the p value and the picture it describes.
  • Comparison designs (Gleicher et al. 2011, "Visual comparison for information visualization"): juxtaposition (two panels side by side: the volcanos now), superposition (one panel, both drawn: the press), explicit encoding (draw the difference itself: a list of which test called which gene). When the difference is the message, explicit encoding reads fastest.
  • Transfer to the lesson's output. 02-4 cell 21 ends in markers.c5 %>% arrange(-avg_log2FC), a gene table, and Two clusters already shows FindMarkers' table. A list with gene names is the form students meet in R; Squair et al. 2021 (Nature Communications, "Confronting false discoveries in single-cell differential expression") make the same case by listing the genes single-cell tests call that pseudobulk does not.
  • Concrete, then general. One gene's cells (concrete) makes the reason visible; the counts and lists (general) show how often it happens. Either order is defensible; the order below follows the overview mantra.
