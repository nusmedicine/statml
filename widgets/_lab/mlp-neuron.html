§1 · The typography

MathML cannot go on a canvas — it is DOM, and this figure is painted. But the thing MathML would buy is a convention anyone can follow with a font: in mathematical typesetting a variable is italic serif and everything that is not a variable — function names, digits, the summation sign — is upright. What the band draws now is the UI sans face with Unicode subscript characters, which is why it reads as a label rather than as mathematics. §2 · The input lines

The lines are shallow because four inputs are squeezed into a band 92px tall and then run ~90px sideways: about 7° of slope, which reads as neither parallel nor converging. The standard fixes, in rough order of how common they are in textbooks:

A is what ships. B spreads the inputs over more height and shortens the run, so the slope roughly doubles — the cheapest fix, and it costs the band nothing. C keeps a short horizontal stub on each input before it turns, which is the engineering-drawing convention: the eye reads a bundle rather than a fan. D curves each line into the sum, the shape most published neuron figures use, and the curvature does the converging so the entry angle stays shallow on purpose. E drops the separate lines entirely: one brace, one arrow, labelled with the dot product — the compact form used when the point is the unit rather than the fan-in.

§3 · What I would ship

Serif italic variables (§1 candidate T2) and lines D. The typography change is free and makes the band read as mathematics rather than as a caption; the curves are what most published diagrams use, they hide the shallow entry angle by construction, and unlike B they do not need the band to grow. E is the one to pick instead if the fan-in is not the point — it is the tidiest of the five and says w·x + b in one mark, but it stops showing that a unit takes many inputs, which is half of what the band is there for.
