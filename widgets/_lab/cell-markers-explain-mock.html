cell-markers · Tumour vs liver: showing why the sample and patient effects matter

Your two questions: can the sample and patient effects be seen, and can the call tiles become a Venn diagram with the truth. Four figures below, all on the widget's own simulation (Kupffer cells, 300 cells a sample, each type its own 20 changed genes). The bar drives all of them. Start at Patient 0.3, Sample 0, then raise the sample effect and watch figure 1's grey bars and figure 2's left histogram.

What the figures draw on

  • Pseudoreplication (Lazic 2010, BMC Neuroscience; Zimmerman, Espeland & Langefeld 2021, Nature Communications, for single-cell data): cells nested in samples nested in patients, and a test that counts the wrong level as its replicates. They explain it with the nesting itself — figure 3.
  • Variance partitioning (Hoffman & Schadt 2016, BMC Bioinformatics, variancePartition): a gene's variation split by where it comes from — patient, sample, cell. Figure 1 does it for one gene, with the simulation's own offsets, which a real study cannot see.
  • The p-value histogram as a calibration check (Breheny, Stromberg & Lambert 2018, High-Throughput): for genes that do not change, an honest test's p values are flat; an overconfident one piles them at 0. Figure 2.
  • Set diagrams (Lex et al. 2014, UpSet, IEEE TVCG): a Venn diagram reads well up to three sets — truth, called over cells, called over samples is three. Fixed circles with counts, not areas, so no area claims a proportion it cannot keep. Figure 4, with the 2 × 2 table (what a DE benchmark prints) as the alternative.

1 · Where one gene's shift comes from

Top: the gene's cells in each sample. Bottom: what moved each sample, as the simulation drew it — the patient offset (the same for a patient's liver and tumour sample), the sample offset (its own), and the true change (tumour samples, truly changed genes only). The lines under it add the parts up for tumour minus liver. The patient offsets appear once on each side, so they subtract away; the sample offsets do not, and with two samples a side they can line up with tissue by chance. Over cells, that chance shift is carried by ~95 cells and looks certain; over samples it is four numbers and looks like what it is.

2 · Across every unchanged gene: are the p values honest?

The p values of the ~410 genes that do not change, over cells and over samples. The dashed line is what an honest test gives (flat: 5% of genes below 0.05 by chance, and adjustment removes those). With a sample effect, the test over cells piles unchanged genes at p ≈ 0 — it counts cells as replicates, so it reads a sample's shift as a real difference. The test over samples stays flat. This is the whole-page version of figure 1.

3 · The design: which level each test counts as a replicate

A static diagram. Each effect lives at one level; each test counts one level as its n. The cell test's n is the bottom row, where cells of one sample share their sample's shift; the pseudobulk's n is the middle row.

4 · The calls against the truth

A · Venn. Three circles: the 20 truly changed genes, and each test's calls. Each dot is a gene — blue truly changed, red unchanged — so a red dot inside a test's circle is a false call, and a blue dot outside both test circles is a gene both missed. Numbers are counts, not areas. B · the 2 × 2 table per test, as a benchmark prints it. The page's four tiles would stay either way, as the text the figure is read against.
