So: no patients, no model, no features — three players and a payout, exactly as 04-5 introduces it. One figure, two halves. On the left your diagram, in motion: the players queue up and walk into the coalition one at a time. On the right the path diagram, drawn by the walking — each arrival adds a node and an edge, so by the end the reader has built the lattice rather than been shown one. Six walks, six routes, and the Shapley value is the average of a column.

The game is a GRID, not a menu. A player's relation to the rest is exactly two things: what it brings alone, and what it creates with somebody. Three settings each is nine cells, and every named case is one of the nine; the sliders underneath reach everything between. The left column is what a linear model looks like — with no pair term the six orderings always agree, and one square right or left they disagree by exactly the size of the pair dividend. That is page 2's central claim, visible as a column.

WHICH pair interacts is the third control, and the three settings are three different lessons, not three repetitions. Put the interaction on A and C or on B and C and the nine numbers are identical — only half the dividend moves to whichever of A and B is now in the group, because C never notices which partner it has, only that it has one. That is symmetry, triggered rather than asserted. Put it on A and B instead and the three columns collapse to one: a dividend you are not in is not yours, whatever its size. The cells rename themselves in each case, so no label ever says “beside A” while the interacting pair is B and C.

“Don't we need the other pairs too?”

Half of that question is now a control — press A and C against B and C and watch the nine numbers not move. A game where B and C interact is a game where A and C interact, relabelled; the same symmetry that makes “A does nothing” and “C does nothing” one case, applied to pairs instead of players. It is checked on screen rather than claimed here.

The other half cannot be a control, and it is the more important one: what about two interactions at once? The grid only ever has one pair live.

It is not a new phenomenon, because the score is LINEAR in the dividends. ϕi is a sum over the groups containing i, and each term is that group's own dividend divided by its size — so turning up A&C does exactly one thing to exactly two scores, and turning up B&C at the same time does its own one thing on top. They never combine into a third effect. That is the notebook's fourth axiom, additivity, and it is the one the other three sections do not reach.

And it is visible in the grid, which is why the cells carry φC as a shade. The nine numbers are an exact plane: every step down a row is −30, every step to the bonus column is +15 and to the penalty column −15, whichever row you are on. No cell deviates. A surface that flat is one you only need two edges of — which is precisely why a two-dial slice teaches the whole seven-number space, and why the sliders underneath are enough for everything in between.

Where the four axioms live on this page, since the notebook states all four and demonstrates none: efficiency is step 5 · symmetry is the complements cell, where the bonus splits evenly · dummy is adds nothing, which scores exactly zero · additivity is the flatness just described.

One group at a time — the general statement

It is not “fix everything except one player” — that is a different and weaker claim. A dividend belongs to a group, not to a player, and what the grid varies is two groups: {C} down the rows and {A,C} across the columns. Varying {B} and {B,C} instead redraws the same nine cells with the letters swapped, because the Shapley value does not know the players' names.

The general statement is simpler than either, and it is the whole theory:

Every game is a stack of one-group games. A one-group game says “this group, all together, creates m” — and its Shapley value is m/k to each of the k members, and zero to everybody else. Scores add across the stack.

Both halves of that are axioms you have already watched: additivity is why the stack adds, symmetry is why the split inside a group is equal. So you never need two groups at once, and two groups of the same size are the same lesson — the only thing that distinguishes a group is its size. Three players have groups of size 1, 2 and 3, so three axes would be a complete picture; the grid carries the first two, and the third splits three ways and teaches no new kind, which is why it is held at zero.

And this is the bridge to page 2. A LINEAR MODEL is exactly the game whose every group of size 2 or more has dividend zero — the grid's left column, generalized. Nothing is ever created jointly, so ϕi is just that feature's own term, every ordering agrees, and the whole apparatus collapses to one multiplication. That is not a coincidence about notebook 04-5; it is what “linear” means in this language.

“So we ADD players, and not subtract them?”

Both — they are the same subtraction seen from opposite ends. Your figure holds a coalition WITH player 1 beside the same coalition WITHOUT, and takes the difference. The walk above starts from the coalition without, lets the player in, and shows the payout move. The step you watch IS your subtraction. The card under the figure prints it in your notation at every arrival, so the two readings are never more than one line apart.

Adding is the right way round for the animation, for two reasons that removal cannot match. One walk produces all three players' contributions at once — three steps, three numbers, one row of the table — where removal needs a separate before-and-after pair for each of the twelve (player, coalition) combinations. And efficiency is only visible on a walk: every route starts at v({ }) and ends at v({A,B,C}), so the steps must sum to the difference. A removal figure has no route in it.

All nine cells, and why nine is the right number

There is no finite set of “all combinations” to enumerate — a three-player game is seven free numbers, so the space is continuous. What makes the grid non-arbitrary is the parameterisation, not the count: any such game is what each player brings alone plus what each pair creates on top (plus a trio term, held at zero here). Those are the Harsanyi dividends, and the Shapley value written in them is one line — every extra is split equally among whoever made it, which is step 6 above. Two dividends, three signs each, nine cells; the sliders are the continuum between them.

Note what does NOT need a cell. “A does nothing” is “C does nothing” with the circles renamed — relabelling players is a symmetry of the game, not a new case, so a widget carrying both would teach one thing twice while looking like it taught two. And φB is 20 in all nine rows below: B is in no dividend that any cell changes, which is the same fact said from the other side.
