§2 · Act 2a — how many bent pieces does the boundary need? (600 epochs, relu)

A single seed is not a measurement, and this act is where that bit. An early draft of this page showed rings k = 3 reaching 0 errors and called 3 "the first k that works"; a different data seed reached 24. Swept over 20 inits per cell — and identically under every optimiser tried, so it is a property of the problem, not the step rule:

          k=1    k=2    k=3     k=4     k=8
rings     0/20   0/20  10/20  19/20  20/20
crescents 0/20   0/20   3/20   6/20  17/20

Two facts, both worth teaching. CAPACITY: k 1 and 2 can never close a ring or thread two arcs — the panels below show a line and a wedge. OPTIMISATION: k 3 sometimes can and usually does not; width buys reliability, not just capacity, because spare units give gradient descent more ways down. So the Seed dial is a teaching control here, and no caption may say "k ≥ 3 works". Blobs are solved at k 1 — a straight line was always enough there, the SVM widget's own opening move.

§4 · The network itself — weights as colour and thickness

Kenneth 2026-08-30: students don't really get weights, so maybe network links can reflect in terms of colour/thickness. The notebook leads its section with exactly this picture. Every edge below is a real fitted weight from the runs above — thickness is |w| (scaled to the largest weight in the net, so the picture is a comparison inside one network) and the three candidates differ only in how they show the SIGN.

A signs by colour, borrowing the stage's two class colours: for the output layer that is literal (a positive weight votes for the red class), for the input layer it means the input excites or inhibits that unit. B uses one ink and shows the sign as solid vs dashed — no colour claim at all. C is colour with the numbers printed, for the reader who does want them.

The diagram explains the k ladder, which is more than decoration. In candidate A above, the fourth hidden unit is a ghost — and measured, it is dead: it fires on 0 of 180 samples, its input weights have norm 0.21 against ~3.0 for the others. The ring is closed by three live units and a corpse. That is the mechanism behind §2's table: rings need three pieces, k = 3 works only when none of the three dies (10/20), k = 4 carries a spare (19/20). A student can SEE why width buys reliability, rather than being told.

And it learns on screen. The same network at four epochs — the edges start near-uniform (random init, largest |w| 0.70) and thicken into the shape that closes the ring (3.17). In the widget this sits beside the boundary panel and moves with it as Play runs.
