§1 · conv2 as combinations of conv1's maps

What is built today, and why it says the first layer twice. Each conv2 map runs one of the same four named operators over ONE pool1 channel. The wiring says a convolution reads every input channel — the figure draws the faint lines for it — and the arithmetic behind it reads one. So the second block shows the reader a horizontal edge of a horizontal edge, which is the first layer's lesson with an extra step.

What a cross-channel kernel is. One conv2 output map carries a 4 × 3 × 3 weight: a 3 × 3 slice per input channel. The four slices are applied to the four pool1 channels at the same position, the results are added, the bias is added, and ReLU follows. The parameter count already pays for the bias — buildNet counts C · out · k · k + out — and the measurement below is the reason it is spent: with four non-negative input channels, a sum of positive weights is a brightness, and the brightest structure carries the largest response until a threshold is subtracted.

How a candidate is measured. The candidate runs through the shipping pipeline — conv1, ReLU, pool1, this kernel, ReLU — and its response is attributed to the parts of the cell that cellParts defines: field, cell body, membrane, nucleus rim, nucleus, granules. A 3 × 3 operator answers a boundary one pixel wide of it on both sides, so the three boundary classes are dilated by one pixel before anything is counted; at the 14 × 14 grid a cell takes the label its four image pixels hold in majority. Enrichment is the share of the map's response mass in a part divided by that part's share of the area, so 1.00 is no preference. The largest it can be is one over the area share, which is printed beside it: the membrane band is a fifth of the picture, so 4.6 is all there is.

§2 · One scan, not two

Why it scans twice today. Behind the gate, one press of Step is three phases. Phase 2 walks the kernel over the chosen layer's input, position by position, with the output map filling in behind the window. Phase 3 then walks the marked unit over every cell of that same map, with the nested receptive-field windows, the cone and the patch on the image moving with it. Both walks visit the same cells of the same map in reading order, and the window phase 2 draws on the immediate source is the innermost window of phase 3's receptive field — the same two numbers, since one step of the backward recursion lo → lo·s − p IS the kernel's own window. So the second walk redraws the first walk's picture and adds the outer windows to it.

The merge. One walk: the marked unit moves a cell at a time in reading order; the output map fills up to that cell; the detail band shows that cell's arithmetic; the windows, the cone and the patch move with it. One press is one cell, one label, one clock. Three frames of it below — the first cell, the middle cell and the last — on pool2, whose 49 cells are large enough to read.

What was drawn twice, and what the merge costs. Shown twice: the cell order, the window on the immediate source, and the output value arriving. Lost: nothing on the figure, and two things in the drive. The two phases had two paces, so a reader could watch the positions slowly and the units quickly; one walk has one pace. And the window in the merged frame carries both readings at once — it is the kernel's own window and the innermost square of the receptive field — where the two phases showed each reading on its own. Gained: the fill and the marked unit are one counter rather than two, so the map behind the unit is always drawn exactly as far as the unit has walked.

The step label. One label, Next unit. The label keyed on the animation's phase goes with the phase it named, which is 4.4b's rule: a label answers what this press will do, and every press now does the same thing.
