cell-qc · is it clustering, or a cut-off? and what would make it obvious

1 · What it is, exactly

your questionthe answer
“there is nearest neighbors?” Yes — but as a local head-count, not as a grouping. For one droplet you take its k nearest neighbours and ask what share of them are made-up doublets. That is a density estimate at a point.
“so it's clustering?” No. Nothing is grouped and no labels come out. Clustering answers which cells belong together; this answers how much company does this one droplet keep with things I made by adding two droplets together. Same ingredient (neighbours), different question. (scDblFinder can optionally generate its artificial doublets cluster-wise, but the scoring is still a neighbourhood count.)
“or just cut-off via distribution?” Yes, at the end — but on an axis that did not exist before. The whole method is a way of manufacturing a number you can threshold, because no threshold on the droplet's own numbers works: the count rules remove none of the 81 doublets, measured. And the cut is usually not chosen by looking for a valley in the score's distribution — DoubletFinder sets it from the expected doublet rate of the machine's loading (nExp, adjusted for the homotypic share), and scDblFinder thresholds to minimise misclassification of its known artificial doublets while staying near that expected rate.

So the honest one-line reading is: make the thing you are looking for, then measure how much each droplet looks like it — and only then threshold. What the widget draws today shows the input (a space with made-up doublets in it) and the output (a score per droplet), and never the step in between, which is where the intuition is. Three candidates for that step:

2 · A · Count the neighbours of one droplet

The score made literally countable: magnify one droplet's neighbourhood and colour its fifty nearest by whether they are real or made up. Two droplets, side by side — one that holds two cells, one that holds one.

For it: it turns an abstract share into a thing you can count, and it is the only view that shows why a droplet scores what it does. A reader who sees this once can read the whole score axis afterwards. Against: it needs two panels and a magnification, and it shows two droplets out of eleven hundred — it explains the method but does not audit it. Best paired with the score rows that are already there.

3 · B · Where a made-up doublet comes from

Before counting neighbours, the reader has to believe the made-up doublets are in the right place. Two real droplets, added, and where the result lands.

For it: it answers the question the current figure begs — where did that grey cloud come from, and why is it in the gaps? Three arrows and the bridges stop being decoration. Against: it is a one-off construction; once understood there is nothing to explore, so it earns its place as a small panel or an opening step, not as a control.

4 · C · The space shaded by how made-up its neighbourhood is

The same count, run everywhere instead of at one droplet: a field over the whole space. The bridges between populations light up, and the real doublets sit in them.

For it: one picture, no magnification, and it shows the score is a property of place — which is exactly the difference from a cut-off on a droplet's own numbers. Against: it is a heat map, so it reads as a continuous surface rather than as a count of fifty things, and the widget's colour roles do not have a second magnitude ramp free (--c-magnitude is violet and shares its slot with highlight). Honest but less countable than A.
