In the ball, or on the sphere

Kenneth asked whether the 3-D distances are Euclidean. They are, exactly — but he asked it as “drawing the links in 3D manifold space”, and widget 22’s samples are not on a manifold. Its stage() puts the four cluster CENTRES on a sphere of radius 2 and then scatters samples around them in all three dimensions, so the cloud fills the ball: radius from the origin runs 1.14 to 3.33, and only 9 of 48 samples land within 0.1 of radius 2. The wireframe globe therefore draws a surface the data is not on.

This page is the alternative, live. Stage B puts every sample exactly on the sphere, as a cap around each cluster direction — a real 2-manifold embedded in three dimensions, which is what cell 46 assumes and what “flattening a globe onto a map” describes. Stage C spreads one continuous band right around it, so there are no clusters to lean on and the curvature is all there is. Everything is computed by widgets/umap/model.js, the module the widget itself runs.

The question the numbers answer. On a curved surface the honest neighbourhood is the GEODESIC one — along the surface — and UMAP only ever sees Euclidean chords through the interior. Cell 46’s claim is that the per-point ρ and σ rescaling recovers the geodesic structure anyway. The right-hand columns test exactly that: neighbours kept against geodesic truth, for the layout and for the chord distances it was given.

What to decide

Does the sphere teach something the ball does not? Look for the gap between the two right-hand columns. If UMAP on chord distances recovers the geodesic neighbourhoods, the manifold assumption is doing real work and the globe is honest. If the chords already agree with the geodesics, the sphere is decoration with extra steps — and dropping the globe is the cheaper fix. Widen the caps to make the curvature matter.
