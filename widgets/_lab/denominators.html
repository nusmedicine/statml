The denominator that moves — four candidates

For widget 12. Risk and odds share a numerator and differ in denominator: risk is deaths over everyone, odds is deaths over the survivors. As the outcome gets common the odds denominator is eaten while the risk denominator is not, so the odds ratio always overstates the risk ratio — and by how much is set by the base rate, not by the effect.

Settled: A, plus the division written out. The question was whether having the survivors step aside reads as the denominator changed or merely as dots moving — everything else in the catalogue entry is checkable arithmetic; this was not. A won, and it now carries the fraction underneath: the same people again as the two terms, ten to a row.

What the fraction adds that the towers cannot. The numerator is the deaths and is identical in both readings. The denominator is everyone, and as the reading moves to odds it sheds its red dots one at a time until only survivors are left. So risk's numerator is visibly inside its denominator and odds' is visibly beside it — which is the reason only one of the two can pass 1, stated as a picture rather than as a rule. At a 30% base rate the exposed group reads 60 ÷ 40 = 1.50, and a risk never could.

B, C and D stay on the page as the record of what was compared, at their original height and without the fraction. Deciding by looking is only honest if the alternatives are still there to look at.

Prior art, surveyed. Every interactive OR/RR tool found is a calculator: type a 2×2, get four numbers. The Rossman/Chance two-way table applet shuffles for a test and does not touch effect size. Nothing animates the denominator. That is candidate D, on the page as the baseline to beat rather than as a straw man — if D reads as well as the others, the widget has not earned its slot.

All four cells run the same numbers in lockstep: 100 people per group and a risk ratio pinned at 2.00, so the effect never changes and every move of the odds ratio is the base rate's doing. The deaths are drawn on the floor and never move — only the denominator does.

  1. At 1%, are the two denominators distinguishable? They should not be — 2 deaths against 100 and 2 against 98 is the rare-outcome approximation, and the whole point is that you can see it rather than be told a threshold. If a candidate makes 100 and 98 look different, it is overselling.
  2. At 40%, does the odds pile read as short? 80 deaths standing beside 20 survivors is where the odds ratio's 6.00 comes from. If the picture does not make that pile look alarming, the number is doing all the work.
  3. In A and C, does the motion read as the denominator changing, or as dots moving? The deaths never move. Watch whether that registers, or whether the eye just follows the travelling block.
  4. Does B's simultaneity beat A's motion? B never moves and shows both readings at once, which is 2.7 — adjacency is the argument. A shows one reading at a time and pays a second for the transition. Only one of these can be the figure.
  5. Do the people earn their space? C is A with the dots replaced by solid bars. If C reads as well, the dots are furniture and 200 of them are expensive furniture.
  6. Is D really worse? Answer this one honestly before reading the others.
  7. Does the fraction's duplication cost more than it buys? Its two terms are the same 100 people already standing in the towers directly above, drawn a second time and smaller. That is the price of the fraction form — you cannot write a division without both terms stacked — but it is a real price, and the alternative is to drop the towers and let the fraction be the whole figure.
  8. Are 100 dots countable, or texture? At this size the numerator block reads as a red rectangle with rows rather than as forty things. 2.3 wants a countable thing; 100 per group is what makes out of 100 a natural frequency. Those two pull opposite ways and this is the size at which to judge which wins — and it wants judging projected, not on a desk.

Token gap this mockup exposes. There is no semantic role for the outcome happened versus it did not. The closest is --c-extreme, which means “past the line: what a p-value counts” — a different idea, and widget 12 has no threshold in it. --c-group-a/--c-group-b are already spoken for by the two arms (exposed / unexposed), which is the other axis of the same table. So this page borrows --series-8 and --series-1 directly, which the house rules forbid in a real widget. If the split survives, this wants deciding once — probably --c-event / --c-nonevent in tokens.css. Note that a 2×2 widget needs both pairs at once, so whatever is added must not collide with the group roles.

Deliberately not on this page: the case-control half. Thinning the survivors 1-in-k is widget 12's second view and its motion is a different question — dots leaving a pile, not a pile moving. It is settled separately, once the split is.
