§0 · The models on the table, measured

The tree is not a strawman — on these three features it out-scores the notebook's own logistic regression, and both out-score the notebook's eleven-feature model on its own test set. So "the complex model" earns its place by being better, which is the only honest reason to want it explained.

§1 · What "the model without this feature" actually means

The notebook writes Δi = f(features with xi) − f(features without xi) and stops. There is no model without a feature. A feature outside the coalition takes its value from somebody else, and v(S) is the average prediction over the whole background. This is the step every SHAP tutorial skips, and it is the one that makes "removing a feature" a thing you can watch. Candidate A shows the stand-ins; candidate B shows only what the formula consumes.

§2 · Six orderings — and the average that is the Shapley value

Patient #50: age 85, ejection fraction 60%, serum creatinine 3.2 mg/dL — and this patient survived. Under the tree, age's marginal contribution runs from +0.357 to −0.543 depending on who is already in the room: the widest disagreement in the whole test set, and a sign flip. Follow one feature down the rows and the averaging stops looking like bookkeeping.

The same eight numbers as a lattice. This is the shape the notebook's formula is literally written over — a sum across subsets S ⊆ N\{i} — and it is where the weight w(S) = |S|!(|N|−|S|−1)!/|N|! stops being arbitrary: it is how many of the six orderings cross that edge, over six. Same numbers, second reading.

§3 · THE COLLAPSE — the notebook's own model is the case where none of this shows

Measured over all 60 test patients. On the notebook's model, on the scale the SHAP library actually returns for it, every one of the six orderings gives the identical number — for every patient, to 1e-15. The coalitions, the weights, the average: all of it collapses to phi_i = beta_i (x_i − xbar_i), which is the closed form shap/explainers/_linear.py computes in one line. Put a sigmoid in front of the same model and the orderings part company; use a tree and they part company hard. That is the control this widget is built around.

§4 · One patient — the waterfall, and the coefficient it is not

Patient #42: age 60, ejection fraction 35%, serum creatinine 1.40 mg/dL. Serum creatinine has the model's largest coefficient (+0.860 per SD) and its attribution for this patient is +0.007 — because 1.40 mg/dL is the training mean to within a hundredth of an SD. A coefficient is per unit of the feature; an attribution is per unit times how far this patient is from average. Candidate B puts the two bars in the same panel so the reader cannot read one as the other.

§5 · Every patient — the beeswarm the notebook prints, and what a row of it is

The notebook's own eleven-feature model, its own shap.plots.beeswarm. Each row is one feature; each dot is one patient's phi, i.e. one bar lifted out of that patient's waterfall. Rows are sorted by mean |phi| — and that order is not the coefficient order: age is 6th by |beta| and 3rd by mean |phi|, because a coefficient is per SD and a binary feature only ever moves by about half of one.
