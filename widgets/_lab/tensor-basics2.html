tensors · Basics — the stage, the index control, and rank 4

Three questions from the first look at the Basics topic, each answered against what the design and education literature settles and then drawn. §1 the stage is cluttered: drawing, print, selection and captions share one canvas with nothing separating them. §2 the index control is four segmented rows in the rail. §3 at rank 4 the stack view and the frames view look alike.

What the literature settles, in three lines. Ainsworth's DeFT framework: a second representation earns its place only when it does a different job from the first (complement), or pins down how the first is to be read (constrain); two that do the same job add cost. Cognitive load theory's split-attention effect: two sources that must be read together must be physically linked, not merely adjacent — the highlight that lights the same value in the drawing and in the print is that link, and it is the reason the print may stay on the stage at all. Gestalt common region and proximity: enclosure groups more strongly than nearness, so a stage carrying more than one representation needs a visible region per representation, with a name. Alammar's and Maximov's NumPy guides, the two most-read visual treatments of arrays, both draw an index as the expression beside the array with the selected cells lit and the result drawn as its own smaller array — the result is a picture, not text.

§1 · The Basics stage — three layouts of the same state, T[:, 0, :] at rank 3

A as built: drawing, print to its right, the selection's print beneath, captions beneath that. B a 2 × 2 of named regions — rows are the tensor and the selection, columns are drawn and printed — so the selection is DRAWN as the sub-tensor the index makes, under the tensor it came from, and each print sits beside the drawing it prints; the row header carries the expression. C the print as a band below the drawing on the second surface colour, the selection drawn beside the tensor in the same view, its print in the band beside the tensor's. In B and C the dimension arrows are gone from the frames view: the edge indices and a roles line name the dimensions, and arrows are kept for the stack view only.

§2 · Entering an index — four ways

a as built: one segmented per dimension, four rows. b the expression itself as the control: one row in the rail reading T[ : , 0 , : ], each slot a small segmented, so the control is the line of code the reader will type; the rail loses three rows. c the figure as the control: the edge indices and frame labels are click targets — click dim 0 = 1 to fix dim 0, click a column index to fix the last dimension, click a cell to fix all three, click the tensor's name to clear — and the rail control stays as the keyboard path (principle 3.6: a click on the figure is a control and obeys every rule a control does). d −1 as an option on every dimension: the lesson's own first index example is T[0, 0, −1], and negative indices are a confusion of their own. a–c can carry it.

§3 · Rank 4 — why stack and frames look alike, and what to do

Any drawing of rank 4 on a page is a 2-D arrangement of 2-D arrangements; the two views differ only in whether the third-from-last dimension is drawn with depth. As built, the stack view at rank 4 puts each exploded stack inside a dashed frame, so the outer dimension is drawn the frames way in BOTH views and the difference shrinks to a diagonal. a as built. b the lesson's own convention: the stack view draws dim 0 as a column DOWN the page — one exploded stack per index, no frames, one arrow — and rank 5 would be a grid of stacks; the frames view keeps its boxes and edge indices. Now the views differ in kind. c a stack of stacks: dim 1 stepped up the diagonal, dim 0 stepped further along the same diagonal, no frames — the fully three-dimensional picture; it does not fit the stage. d not drawn: offer the view toggle at rank 3 only, where depth means something, and draw rank 4 one way.
