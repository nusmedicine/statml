Why this order, and what the evidence says

  • Guide by what exists, not by instructions. PhET’s implicit scaffolding is the idea that guidance should live in the affordances and constraints of the thing itself, so students are “guided without feeling guided”. That is an argument for revealing controls rather than printing a numbered list of steps — which is what this mock-up does, and why there is no “now do this” text anywhere in the rail. (Podolefsky, Perkins & Moore, 2013)
  • Each stage relaxes a constraint. PhET’s own description of successive tabs is that they “add complexity… and sometimes relax constraints present in earlier tabs, e.g. by allowing control over more variables simultaneously”. Stages 1–5 here do exactly that, one control at a time, and every control stays once revealed. (PhET research)
  • Seeing the contrast first is what makes the explanation land. Schwartz & Bransford’s A Time For Telling finds that explicit instruction is far more effective when learners have first been prepared by comparing contrasting cases — and Schwartz & Martin found the same in statistics instruction specifically. Stage 1 showing the whole cohort before stage 2 takes it away is that contrast: the reader watches 142 of 150 cases disappear rather than being told the class is rare. (Schwartz & Martin, 2004)
  • Autonomy without structure backfires. The same literature is clear that handing over every control at once produces frustration rather than discovery. The current widget opens with five controls and three buttons visible at once.
